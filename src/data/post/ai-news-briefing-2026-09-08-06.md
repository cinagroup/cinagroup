## Top 7 Stories

### 1. Seattle Times and Newsday Are the Latest to Sue OpenAI and Microsoft
TechCrunch reports that the Seattle Times and Newsday have filed suit against OpenAI and Microsoft, alleging the companies used their journalism to train AI systems without authorization. The two outlets join a growing list of news organizations — including The New York Times, Chicago Tribune, and others — pressing copyright claims against the AI industry in court.

The suits land at a delicate moment for OpenAI's media strategy: the company is simultaneously signing licensing and partnership deals with publishers, defending itself against existing litigation, and now facing a widening ring of regional and local newsroom plaintiffs. For Microsoft, the cases add pressure on the company's role as OpenAI's largest backer and the distributor of AI tools across its products.

### 2. OpenAI, AIRPPU and WAN-IFRA Launch AI Program for Ukrainian Journalism
OpenAI announced Monday a joint program with AIRPPU and WAN-IFRA to help Ukrainian news organizations strengthen innovation, resilience, and independent journalism through AI. The initiative is the company's latest move into journalism support, arriving in the same week as new publisher lawsuits against it.

The timing is striking: even as OpenAI litigates against US outlets over training data, it is positioning itself as a defender of independent media abroad. Expect the Ukraine program to feature prominently in the company's argument that it is a partner to journalism — and to draw scrutiny from publishers who say the same technology is being deployed without permission at home.

### 3. Authors Push Back as Publishers Stake Claims on Anthropic Settlement
TechCrunch reports that authors are pushing back as publishers and literary agents make claims on the Anthropic copyright settlement, saying publishers appear to be taking more than their fair share of the payments. The dispute centers on how settlement proceeds are divided among the parties whose works were allegedly used to train Anthropic's models.

The fight exposes the fault lines inside the copyright wars: authors and publishers are technically aligned against AI companies, but they are also competitors for the same compensation. If publishers are seen to be capturing settlement money that belongs to individual writers, it could fracture the coalition of rights-holders that has driven the litigation wave — and complicate the template for future AI copyright deals.

### 4. Google's Gemini Spark Can Now Manage Your Google Photos Library
Google's Gemini Spark assistant can now edit and curate photo albums, create shared collections, turn photos into calendar events, and handle other Google Photos tasks, according to TechCrunch. The capabilities are rolling out to AI Pro and Ultra subscribers, extending Gemini's reach into one of Google's most personal consumer products.

This is a meaningful step in the consumer-agent race: photo libraries are deeply personal data, and handing an AI the ability to reorganize them is a trust milestone. For Google, it turns Photos from a storage product into a demonstration surface for agentic AI; for users, it raises the stakes on accuracy and privacy — one bad edit of a shared family album is a very visible failure.

### 5. Meta Will Pay to See How You Use Its New Muse Spark Model
TechCrunch reports that Meta is offering an explicit discount averaging about 95% for users of its new Muse Spark model — built for operating coding and other agents — who "contribute" to future model development by sharing their prompts and outputs. The program essentially pays users in compute credits for telemetry on how the model is actually used.

The arrangement is a bid to bootstrap an ecosystem around Muse Spark by turning every discounted user into a data source for the next generation. But it also crystallizes the industry's data economics: as frontier training data grows scarce, labs are increasingly treating real-world usage logs as the new training fuel — and Meta is making the trade explicit rather than implicit.

### 6. Travis Kalanick's Atoms May Be Getting Into Robotaxis
The Uber founder's robotics startup Atoms is reportedly exploring the robotaxi business, per TechCrunch, with Kalanick saying the company will allow him to complete "unfinished business." Atoms, which has been building delivery and logistics robots, would enter a market already crowded by Waymo, Tesla, and a wave of Chinese operators.

Kalanick's history makes the move provocative: he built Uber in part on the promise of autonomous ride-hailing, a vision that never materialized under his tenure. A return to robotaxis through Atoms would be one of the most closely watched second acts in the industry — and a signal that the unit economics of autonomous mobility are finally attracting new entrants.

### 7. Hikers Rescued After Following Google Gemini's Planning Advice
A sheriff's office says hikers were rescued after a mountain outing planned with Google Gemini, reporting that the group "were advised by Gemini to bring far less food and water than their group required," per TechCrunch. The hikers survived, but the incident has become a viral cautionary tale about AI-generated advice in high-stakes physical settings.

The story matters beyond the headline: it is a concrete example of the trust gap between AI's confident output and its lack of grounding in physical reality. As assistants like Gemini are pushed into everyday decision-making — route planning, meal prep, health advice — incidents like this sharpen the question of where AI suggestions should stop and human judgment should take over.
