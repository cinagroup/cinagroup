## Top 7 Stories

### 1. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind Are Reportedly Working Together on AI Safety

OpenAI is working with rivals Anthropic and Alphabet's Google DeepMind on AI safety, Bloomberg News reported, citing the ChatGPT maker's global policy chief Chris Lehane. The disclosure is notable because it puts the three leading frontier labs nominally in the same room on safety questions at a moment when each is racing the others toward more capable models.

The collaboration arrives amid mounting antitrust scrutiny of the same companies. Gizmodo reported that some AI startup executives — including Cohere CEO Aidan Gomez — are portraying industry calls for safety coordination as a covert way to entrench incumbents by shaping rules that smaller competitors cannot afford to meet.

The story sits at the center of an increasingly crowded debate: whether voluntary cooperation among frontier labs is a genuine safety mechanism or a form of regulatory capture dressed up as caution.

### 2. Nvidia's Jensen Huang Calls the Innovation-Versus-Safety Fight a 'False Choice'

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said the battle over AI innovation and safety is a "false choice," arguing that no new US laws or regulations are needed to control AI development. His position lands squarely against the drift of the safety debate, where Anthropic's leadership and others have called for guardrails.

Huang's stance carries weight well beyond Nvidia's own products, since the company's hardware underlies most of the frontier training runs in question. It also aligns him with a deregulatory posture in Washington, where the Trump administration has been batting down calls for new AI rules.

The remarks underline a widening split in the industry: labs publicly urging caution while the infrastructure layer argues that caution, translated into law, would be the more dangerous choice.

Source: [Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/2e20c674-3386-46ad-b07f-5cd8a013f67e)

### 3. Meta's Muse AI Agent Becomes the No. 2 App in the US

Meta's new AI agent, Muse, has climbed to the No. 2 spot among apps in the United States, according to TechCrunch, positioning it as a direct challenge to Google. Muse is designed to act on a user's behalf — booking travel and making purchases — extending Meta's push to turn AI from a chat surface into a transactional one.

Analysts welcomed the launch, sending Meta shares up roughly 5% as the product rolled out. The framing matters: Meta is positioning Muse as a personal shopper and agentic assistant, leaning on its existing distribution to drive adoption.

The open question is trust. Given Meta's history on data practices, reviewers have questioned whether users will hand over purchase and travel authority to an agent operated by the company — a hesitation that will shape how quickly Muse converts its early ranking into durable usage.

### 4. Asia's Chip Challengers Raise Big Rounds Targeting Nvidia's Networking Dominance

Cornelis, an AI infrastructure company building networking technology to help AI chips communicate more effectively, raised $205 million in a bid to chip away at Nvidia's dominance. Separately, Delos Data — founded by Intel veterans — raised $100 million to develop chips and software for AI data-center networks.

The two rounds point to where the next competitive front is forming. As training and inference clusters scale, the interconnect between accelerators becomes as decisive as the accelerators themselves, and that is precisely the layer Nvidia has locked down through its networking portfolio.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's own stock fell alongside Broadcom and AMD on the prospect of an AI slowdown, though Barron's argued the company remains the best-shielded chip bet in that scenario.

### 5. Washington Splits Over AI Regulation as Fears Spike

President Trump is downplaying mounting concerns about artificial intelligence and resisting calls for regulation, The Hill reported, even as the debate over the technology's risks intensifies. Vice President JD Vance went further, calling tech leaders' push for government regulation a "Trojan horse" designed to crush competition.

The politics are unusually tangled. Senator Ted Cruz — long a critic of AI regulation — is now positioned to help shape the federal response to a viral warning from Anthropic, according to the Houston Chronicle. And on the right, commentators are framing the regulatory push as an artifact of the "effective altruism" movement backed by billionaire Dustin Moskovitz.

On the other side, an AI policy expert at the Abundance Institute dismissed Big Tech's regulation calls outright, telling Fox News viewers that "we should see through this." The through-line across all of it: no consensus exists in Washington on whether the answer to AI risk is more rules or fewer.

### 6. China Pushes Back on Anthropic's Call to Slow AI Development

A Chinese state newspaper blasted Anthropic's calls to slow AI development as a "Cold War" tactic, Reuters reported, after an essay by Anthropic's CEO urged a slowdown. The rebuke escalates a rhetorical fight that has been building for weeks.

Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark countered that the US loses to China by "rolling the dice" on AI safety, arguing the competitive framing is itself the risk. His position was echoed — and complicated — by an OpenAI researcher, Daniel Selsam, who broke ranks to argue that pacing the frontier, as both Sam Altman and Dario Amodei suggest, will not be enough to avoid catastrophic outcomes.

Notably, OpenAI president Greg Brockman has said the company has already put some cutting-edge projects on ice over safety concerns, a claim that pushes back on the idea that labs are doing nothing while warning about risk.

### 7. Enterprise AI Agents Hit a Trust and Governance Wall

Enterprise leaders are increasingly worried about intellectual property leaking to AI labs, a challenge Forbes framed as the enterprise AI trust crisis — and one answer emerging in response is governed agent memory. The idea: give agents persistent context without handing the underlying data to the model providers.

A parallel thread is emerging around action permissions. As AI agents move from answering questions to taking actions on behalf of humans — sometimes entirely autonomously — enterprises need a new data rule book before they deploy, Forbes' tech council argued. Vendors are already moving into the gap: ARTI Analytics launched ARTI Dominion, an independent control layer intended to govern how models and agents act inside an organization.

The geographic spread is telling too. ByteDance is weaving AI agents into its Feishu workplace software as competition with Tencent and Alibaba intensifies, while Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff described a "huge opportunity" for India as the company rebuilds around AI-first enterprise systems.
