### 1. Nvidia's Jensen Huang Crisis Deepens as Allies Build a "War Room"

The reporting frames the board's core issue not as the relationships themselves but as the perceived dishonesty surrounding them.

Meanwhile, allies of Huang have assembled what Ars Technica describes as a "war room" aimed at returning him to the company, and Huang himself has reportedly expressed a wish to come back. Critics, per Ars, have pushed back sharply, with one line of attack warning that "Trump was in bed with Jensen," tying the comeback effort to Huang's political entanglements.

The episode has become the defining story of the AI-chip world this week, with Wired's coverage extending to the blast radius hitting Huang's favorite restaurants in Taiwan and fueling anxieties among tech workers. For Nvidia, the leadership vacuum lands at an awkward moment — just as the broader AI-infrastructure trade faces questions about sustainability.

### 2. Google Removes the Search Button in Homepage AI Redesign

Sources: [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/11/googles-latest-search-page-redesign-drops-the-search-button-adds-ai-sidebar/)

Google has rolled out a new search homepage that drops the traditional search button and adds an AI-powered sidebar carrying chat history, according to Ars Technica. It is the most visible change to Google's core product surface in years, shifting the default interaction model from typing a query into a box toward continuing a conversation with an AI assistant.

The redesign folds prior AI-chat threads into the search experience, letting users pick up earlier conversations directly from the homepage. Ars frames the move as Google betting that conversational history, not one-shot queries, is the future of its flagship product — a direct response to assistant-first rivals.

The change carries substantial stakes for publishers and advertisers who depend on the search box as the primary funnel to the open web, and it lands as Google separately faces scrutiny over AI in schools following its Gemini pilot backlash.

### 3. Major US Banks Move to Ban Third-Party AI Agents From Accounts

Sources: [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/banks-are-cracking-down-on-ai-agents/)

Major US financial institutions are banning third-party AI bots from logging into customer accounts and warning that any costly mistakes made by those agents are strictly the customer's responsibility, Wired reports. The policy shift targets a fast-growing class of consumer "agentic" tools that log in on a user's behalf to move money, pay bills, or manage accounts.

The banks' stance draws a hard line on liability: if an autonomous agent mishandles a transaction or is manipulated by a scammer, the institution will not absorb the loss. That effectively pushes the risk onto consumers and the agent developers who built the tools.

The move is an early concrete example of financial institutions setting the rules for AI agents before regulators do, and it previews the friction that agentic commerce will face across other regulated sectors where liability and identity verification are non-negotiable.

### 4. Meta Reconsiders 2030 Net-Zero Goal Amid Record AI Data-Center Emissions

Sources: [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/11/meta-reconsiders-2030-net-zero-goal-amid-record-emissions-from-ai-data-centers/)

Meta is reconsidering its 2030 net-zero emissions commitment as its carbon footprint hits record levels driven by AI data-center buildout, Ars Technica reports. The company had previously positioned the 2030 target as a headline climate pledge; the reassessment signals that the energy demands of training and serving frontier models are colliding with those promises.

The story fits a wider pattern across hyperscalers, where the power and cooling requirements of GPU clusters have pushed emissions in the wrong direction even as companies invest heavily in renewable procurement. Reopening a public net-zero goal is politically sensitive and invites scrutiny from regulators and investors who treat climate commitments as material disclosures.

The disclosure also sharpens the climate-vs-compute tension at the heart of the AI boom: the same infrastructure driving revenue growth is the primary obstacle to meeting sustainability targets.

### 5. Meta's "Muse" AI Assistant Draws Privacy Backlash — Internally and on Instagram

Sources: [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/muse-is-unleashing-a-world-of-pettiness-on-instagram/) · [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/11/muse-privacy-invasions-are-freaking-out-meta-staffers-internal-chats-show/)

Meta's AI assistant Muse is generating controversy on two fronts. Wired reports that Muse can track and unfollow Instagram users who don't follow you back — a "pettiness" feature that some users are embracing enthusiastically. Separately, Ars Technica reports that internal chats show Muse's privacy invasions are alarming Meta's own staffers.

The combination is notable: a consumer-facing feature that automates social score-settling, built atop data practices that employees themselves find troubling. It puts Meta at the center of the recurring debate over how much behavioral data an assistant should be allowed to act on autonomously.

Both stories underscore that AI assistants embedded in social platforms face a different bar than standalone chatbots — users expect them to mediate relationships, and the privacy and ethical edges of that role are far from settled.

### 6. OpenAI Targets "Adversarial Distillation" as Model-Copying Fight Escalates

Sources: [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/) · [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence)

OpenAI published a policy and technical position on combating "adversarial distillation" — the practice of extracting a frontier model's capabilities by training a rival model on its outputs. The company frames it as a way for competitors to free-ride on expensive research while sidestepping safety and usage terms.

The post is part of a broader OpenAI push on how intelligence gets produced and protected: a companion essay titled "Perplexity and Intelligence" examines the trade-offs of capability versus reliability, published the same weekend. Together they signal OpenAI positioning itself on the defensive side of the model-provenance debate.

The distillation issue is becoming a central competitive and legal battleground, as labs seek technical and contractual defenses against model copying while regulators weigh whether output-based training is fair use or misappropriation.

### 7. US Moves to Cut China's Remote Access to AI Chips

Sources: [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/11/us-moves-to-cut-chinas-remote-access-to-ai-chips/)

The US is moving to close a loophole that lets Chinese entities access advanced AI chips remotely — for example via cloud instances — even when they cannot buy the hardware directly, Ars Technica reports. The action extends export-control logic from physical shipments to remote compute access.

The step reflects a growing recognition in Washington that controlling where chips physically land is insufficient if the compute can be rented from abroad. Enforcement, however, is technically difficult, since it requires tracking who is on the other end of a cloud session.

The move is likely to reshape how cloud providers structure overseas offerings and to intensify the broader US-China competition over AI compute, which remains the scarcest input in frontier model development.
